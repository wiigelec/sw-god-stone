## The Star That Moved Wrong

On the night of the High Count, the whole city climbed the temple terraces to watch the sky take its proper shape.

They came in silence at first: farmers with river mud still dark on their ankles, bronze-smiths smelling faintly of coal smoke, mothers carrying sleeping children, old men leaning on carved sticks polished by generations of hands. They filled the lower steps and the moon court and the long eastern stair, all of them facing upward toward the black bowl of heaven.

Above them, on the highest terrace, Nari knelt beside the seeing-stone and rubbed lampblack into the tablet grooves.

The work was humble. Her fingers were stained to the first knuckle. Fine soot had gathered under one nail, and the wool cloth in her lap was gray from polishing the old lens. But Master Orem had trusted her with the lens on the night of the High Count, and that mattered more than clean hands.

No one knew who had made the lens.

It sat in a ring of dark metal at the terrace edge, round as a water jar’s mouth and clear only when the lamps were hooded. By day it looked smoky and dead. By night, if cleaned properly, it gathered the heavens and made them sharp enough to wound.

“Ready,” Master Orem said.

Nari lowered her head. “Ready, master.”

The high priest lifted his bronze rod.

Below, the city stilled.

Even the children seemed to feel the old hush. The High Count was not merely a ritual. It opened the planting days. It fixed the river tolls. It told the king when to sit in judgment and when to keep his doors shut. Marriages were delayed for it. Funerals waited for it. If the sky was read wrongly, the city would spend a year walking out of step with the gods.

Master Orem pointed the rod toward the eastern dark.

“The Ox Horn,” he called.

“The Ox Horn holds,” answered the crowd.

Nari bent over the lens. The star was where it should be, low and red over the hills. She marked the tablet.

“The Bowl of Mothers.”

“The Bowl receives.”

She found the curve of pale lights and marked them.

“The River Chain.”

“The River binds.”

One by one, the sacred lights came into their appointed places. Master Orem named them, the city answered, and Nari confirmed each point through the seeing-stone. Her hand moved with the rhythm of it: look, find, mark; look, find, mark. She had practiced until she could do the lower counts half asleep, but tonight she let herself feel no pride. Pride made the hand careless. Carelessness made false marks. False marks brought famine, or so the old women said.

Master Orem’s rod shifted north.

“The Gate of Ash.”

“The Gate remains.”

Nari looked.

The Gate of Ash was there: three cold lights in a bent line above the black shoulder of the western ridge.

She touched her stylus to the wax.

Then the middle light blinked.

Her hand stopped.

Smoke, she thought.

A lamp had smoked. Or her eye had watered. Or the lens, old and temperamental, had caught some flaw in its own depths. Nari lifted the wool cloth and wiped the stone again, slow circles from center to rim, as Master Orem continued beside her.

“The White Jar.”

“The White Jar pours.”

Nari did not mark it.

She looked back to the Gate of Ash.

The middle light blinked again.

Once. Twice. A pause. Once. Twice.

Then it shifted.

Not far. Not enough for the people below to gasp or point. It moved the width of a seed held at arm’s length, westward against the star-law Nari had copied since childhood.

Her mouth went dry.

Master Orem’s rod clicked softly against the stone as he lowered it. The sound meant warning.

Nari realized she had made a noise.

“Attend,” he murmured without looking at her.

“The Gate moved,” she whispered.

“The Gate remains.”

“It moved wrong.”

His face did not change, but the skin tightened at the corner of his jaw. “The heavens do not move wrong.”

She looked again. The light held still now, innocent among its sisters.

For a moment shame flooded her so quickly that she almost bowed over the tablet and begged forgiveness. She was tired. The climb had been long. The lamps were too close. Hundreds watched from below, and she was only an apprentice with soot on her fingers.

Then the light blinked three times.

Not twice. Three.

Pause.

Three again.

Nari’s fear changed shape.

Bad omens were wild. Comets tore. Fire-stars fell. The moon bled. Storm halos crawled around the faces of the gods.

This was not wild.

This kept count.

She cleaned the lens a third time, though it was already clear. She checked the tablet grooves. She counted the fixed stars around the Gate. She placed the bronze divider between the marks and measured the little westward theft of distance.

It had moved.

And now, through the seeing-stone, a thin line of pale light had appeared where no line had ever been.

It ran from the Gate of Ash down through the dark of the lens, crossing the star-map not as a scratch upon the stone but as a reflection buried inside it. Nari leaned closer, hardly breathing. The line trembled once with the blinking of the star, then extended past the rim of the lens and touched the terrace floor.

No one else seemed to see it.

The crowd answered Master Orem’s next call. Their voices rose and fell together, obedient as tidewater.

“The Crown of First Fire.”

“The Crown endures.”

The pale line crept across the stone between Nari’s knees.

It passed over the carved circle of the moon court. It crossed the old drain channel. It reached the black seam at the base of the inner wall, where the forbidden stair lay hidden behind seven fitted stones.

Nari’s heart struck once, hard.

The lower temple.

No apprentice entered there. No lamp was carried there except on the winter sealing. No name was spoken over its threshold. Even Master Orem went down only with the elder priests, barefoot and fasting, and came back gray-faced before dawn.

Beside her, he said, “Mark the Crown.”

Nari dipped her stylus, but her hand would not move.

“Nari.”

She looked up at him.

For the first time that night, Master Orem was not watching the sky. He was watching her. The bronze rod lay still in his hand. In the hooded lamplight, his face looked carved and old and afraid.

“You saw it,” she whispered.

His voice was softer than the crowd below, softer than the scrape of her own breath. “Mark the Crown.”

“The Gate is answering something.”

His fingers closed around her wrist.

Not hard enough to bruise. Hard enough to command.

“There is no answer,” he said. “There is only the Count.”

Below them, the city waited for the next name.

Above them, the middle light of the Gate of Ash blinked three times.

Pause.

Three times.

Nari looked from the star to the pale line on the terrace floor, from the pale line to the black seam in the inner wall. The ritual lamps burned steady around her. The crowd stood wrapped in certainty. Master Orem’s hand held her in the old order.

But beneath the stone, something had begun to count back.

Nari slipped her wrist free.

She marked nothing.

While Master Orem raised his rod and called the next sacred name, she gathered the wool cloth, the bronze divider, and the little temple key she was not supposed to know he kept under the tablet box.

Then she stepped backward out of the ring of lamplight and went toward the forbidden stair.
