The temple emptied slowly after the High Count failed.

For a while there had been too many sounds: sandals dragging over stone, old men whispering prayers too quickly, the bronze chains of the counting lamps ringing where frightened hands had struck them. Then the priests drove the last of the watchers down from the court, and the great upper chamber settled into a silence that felt less like peace than shame.

Nari stayed where the shadow of the western pillar crossed the floor.

No one looked for her. That was one mercy of being an apprentice. In the hour of failure, the masters saw only one another.

The last lamp nearest the count-table guttered blue at its rim. Melted fat had run down the clay side and hardened there in pale threads. The chalk marks she had scratched into the black counting slate still waited under her sleeve, hidden against her ribs where she had tucked the shard away before Master Orem could see exactly what she had written.

Not a mistake.

She pressed her palm against the slate until its edge hurt.

Not cloud. Not trembling glass. Not fear.

The star had moved wrong, and the count had broken to match it.

From the far side of the court came the soft scrape of a door bar lowered into place. Nari held her breath. A junior acolyte crossed the passage beyond the screen, carrying two dead lamps and muttering the closing prayer in pieces. His voice faded down the outer stair.

Only then did she move.

She wrapped the counting slate in the fold of her sash. The little lens went into the pouch beneath it. Her fingers wanted to shake, so she gave them work: knot, fold, tuck, press flat. Master Orem had taught her that fear was allowed in the body but not in the hands. He had said it when she was nine and too small to reach the high sighting rail without a box.

Master Orem had also told her never to enter the lower ways.

Nari looked toward the prayer-screen behind the western pillar.

Every apprentice knew the screen was loose. Every apprentice pretended not to know. Behind it was a narrow service passage used for oil jars, ash pots, and the small repairs that kept the holy places appearing untouched by human need. Twice each winter Nari had helped carry spent wicks through that passage, eyes down, mouth closed, while an older girl hissed the warnings at her ear.

Never after dark.

Never below the third turning.

Never touch the old stair.

The God-Stone rests there, the girl had whispered, and what rests beneath the gods is not for breath.

Nari slipped behind the pillar and laid both hands against the screen. The carved cedar was warm from the day’s lamps. The prayers cut into it were worn shallow by generations of palms. She found the gap at the bottom, lifted, and felt the screen loosen with its familiar tired sigh.

Dust breathed out.

She waited. No call came. No sandal scraped.

Nari slid through.

The passage behind the screen was too narrow for ceremony. Its ceiling forced her head down. Oil had blackened the walls in long hand-height smears, and the smell of old smoke lay thick over salt, mouse droppings, and damp stone. Here the temple lost its gold voice. Here it was hooks, jars, cracked plaster, broom straw, and the small blunt objects of labor.

She welcomed that at first. Workplaces obeyed rules. Jars could be counted. Steps could be measured. Shadows could be followed.

At the first turning she passed the shelf where winter wicks were stored. At the second, a bronze ring hung from the ceiling for the raising of lamp chains in the outer court. At the third, the passage widened just enough for a person to turn back.

Beyond it, the stair descended.

Nari stopped.

The first three steps were the same rough gray stone as the upper temple. The fourth was darker. The fifth had no chisel marks at all.

She should have turned back then. She knew this with a clarity so sharp it was almost calm. She should have gone to the apprentice room, lain down without sleeping, and waited for morning to make cowardice look like obedience.

Instead, she set one foot on the fourth step.

The air changed below the third turning. It grew cooler but not fresher. The damp smell thinned, and beneath it came something dry and mineral, like stone split open under frost. Her lamp was small, no more than a thumb flame cupped in horn, but its light seemed to travel farther than it should. The stair walls returned it in narrow lines.

Nari touched one.

Smooth.

Not polished by hands. Not worn by robes. Smooth as still water, and cold enough that her fingertip ached.

She took another step, then another, counting because her mind did it before she could forbid it. Seven steps. Seven again. A landing. Fourteen steps. A landing. Seven marks cut into the left wall, then fourteen on the right, then a space where the next mark should have been but was not.

She lifted the lamp.

The carvings had changed.

Above, the prayers wandered as hands wandered: a deep stroke where a grieving father had cut too hard, a crooked blessing from a year of famine, a child’s name hidden under the wing of a holy bird. Here the marks had no such mercy in them. They repeated with a patience that made her throat close. Lines, cups, pierced circles, hooked strokes. Not words. Not any prayer she knew.

A count.

No. She swallowed. Maybe an old mason’s measure. Maybe a foundation record. Maybe some buried priesthood sign no apprentice had been taught.

She tried to make the lie stand upright in her mind.

It fell.

At the next landing, a strip of black showed beneath a flake of plaster. Nari bent close. The plaster had been painted with blue stars and the white hands of the sky-god, but under the god’s wrist the black surface waited, unmarked and faintly shining. Her lamp flame trembled over it.

No soot clung there.

She reached toward it, stopped, whispered the first words of the threshold prayer, and hated herself for needing them.

“Let the count be clean.”

The black strip answered.

Not with sound. Not with movement.

A narrow point of light opened inside it, small as a seed.

Nari dropped the lamp.

The horn case struck the stair and rolled. The flame shuddered, shrank, then held. Nari lunged for it on her knees, one hand over her mouth to keep the cry in. The point in the wall had already gone dark.

For a long while she crouched there, breathing through her fingers.

A trick of the lamp, she told herself.

But the lamp had fallen after.

She picked it up. Her palm was slick against the handle. She wanted Master Orem. She wanted him so badly that anger rose under the wanting. He would have told her to leave. He would have told her the eye invents patterns when the heart is proud. He would have told her to trust the sanctioned count.

He had seen the star.

He had seen it and turned away.

Nari stood.

The stair ended at a door that was not a door. There was no frame, no hinge, no bar, only a dark seam in a wall of stone so smooth the lamp made a second flame in it. Around the seam, later hands had done what hands always did when they were afraid: they had painted prayers. Red circles. White eyes. Lines of warding script. Knotted cords stiff with old oil hung from bronze pins hammered badly into stone that had not wanted them.

At the center of the painted prayers was the mark of the God-Stone.

Nari knew it from every festival cloth and every counting bowl: the black oval with seven rays beneath, fourteen above, and the open eye at its heart.

Here the eye had been painted over the seam.

She lifted her free hand. Her fingers hovered a breath away.

If she touched it, she could no longer pretend she had only wandered. If it opened, she could never unknow that it could. If it did nothing, perhaps she could still crawl backward into the world as it had been.

The wrong star moved again in her memory, patient and impossible.

Nari touched the eye.

Cold ran through the wall.

The seam widened without a scrape.

She did not move. The wall did. One half withdrew from the other with such silence that the prayers painted across it split cleanly down their strokes. Darkness opened beyond.

The air that came out was old, but not dead.

Nari stepped through.

The chamber below the temple was larger than the court above it.

Her lamp could not find the ceiling. Its little light showed a floor falling away in shallow rings, each ring cut with channels so fine they caught the flame and bent it. Pillars stood where no pillar should have been needed, too slender for the weight above, too exact in their spacing. Some had been wrapped in prayer cloth. Others had been plastered over and painted with the faces of gods. The plaster had cracked. Under the cracks was blackness.

At the chamber’s center rose the underside of the God-Stone.

Nari had seen its upper face all her life: the sacred black mass set into the floor of the inner sanctuary, veiled except during the High Count, touched only by the master of measures. From above it was a stone, holy because it had fallen from the first night and given the sky its numbers.

From below, it was not stone.

She had no word for what it was.

A dark curve hung through the chamber roof, vast and smooth, as if a piece of night had been pressed down into the earth and the temple built around the wound. Its surface held no chisel line. No vein. No grain. Faint light moved inside it, slow and deep, not on the surface but within, like a thought passing behind a closed eye.

Offerings lay everywhere beneath it.

Bowls of salt. Strips of bronze. Little clay birds. Children’s teeth wrapped in red thread. The dead prayers of centuries heaped at the foot of a thing that had never asked for them.

Nari took one step down into the ringed floor.

Her sandal made no sound.

Not carved, she thought.

She raised the lamp.

Not grown.

The faint inner light shifted.

Not stone.

Her knees weakened so suddenly she had to grip one of the slender pillars. The cloth wrapped around it crumbled under her hand. Beneath the rotted prayer-strip the pillar was not round but many-sided, its edges too fine, its coldness immediate against her skin.

She began to count because there was nothing else left that belonged to her.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.

The chamber remained still.

Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.

A pulse moved through the floor.

Nari stopped.

The pulse came again, not under her feet only but through the pillar, through her palm, through the bones of her wrist. It was not a sound, though the air seemed to lean toward it. It was not speech. It corrected the silence.

Seven.

A pause.

Fourteen.

A pause where no pause belonged.

Then one more.

Nari could not breathe.

That was the broken place. The impossible interval from the High Count. The place where Master Orem’s hand had faltered and the watching priests had begun to pray too loudly. The place where the star had stepped aside from heaven’s road as if answering a number no one had spoken.

On the underside of the God-Stone, a single point of light opened.

It was no larger than a grain at first. Then the darkness around it thinned, and Nari saw a line, then a curve, then a scatter of smaller lights arranged in an arc she knew better than her own name. The winter road. The Jackal’s Cup. The three lamps of the High Gate.

And there, just off the path where nothing should burn, the wrong star.

It moved.

Not across the surface. Within it.

The light inside the God-Stone made the same broken step she had seen in the sky.

Nari backed away until her heel struck the next ring of the floor. The lamp flame jerked. Shadows leapt up the wrapped pillars, and for a heartbeat every painted god in the chamber seemed to turn its cracked face toward her.

Above, a door closed.

The sound traveled down the stair, small and human and terrible.

Nari froze.

Another sound followed: sandals on stone.

Slow. Measured. Not the hurried tread of an acolyte sent for oil. Not the shuffling panic of a frightened worshipper. A priest’s step, trained to make even descent sound like ritual.

The chamber pulsed again.

Seven. Fourteen. Broken.

Nari closed her hand over the lamp until the horn shade bit her fingers. She looked toward the split door, then back at the God-Stone. The wrong star still burned beneath its skin. The point of light held its place as if waiting for her to finish the count.

From above, Master Orem’s voice came down into the dark.

“Nari.”

He did not call loudly.

He did not sound surprised.

The chamber answered once beneath her feet.
