# The Answering Light

Orem appeared in the passage with one hand braced against the wall. The fourth white mark still shone.

Nari stood beyond the pale floor line, closer to the marked wall and the God-Stone than to the exit. The shortest path to Orem crossed the line she had tested. She could go around its end along the wall, but that meant staying in the chamber longer. Orem took in the distance between them, the line, and the marks on the wall, and swore.

Not loudly. Not like a priest. Like a man who had dropped something heavy on his own foot.

“Don't cross that.”

Nari looked at the marks. “It didn't wait for you to cross.”

Orem's eyes went to the wall. “I know.”

“Then the line isn't what starts it.”

“I don't know what starts it.” He swallowed. “Come around the end. Slowly.”

“I already crossed it. Once. Then back.”

His face changed. “And it answered?”

“The marks started. They stopped when I crossed back.”

“Gods.”

The fifth mark appeared while neither of them was moving.

“That isn't what it did before,” Nari said.

“I can see that. Move.”

He stepped into the chamber, then stopped himself before the pale strip. The passage remained open behind him. Three paces of ordinary-looking floor separated him from the line; another several separated the line from Nari. Beyond her, the God-Stone descended black and seamless through the ceiling into its pale ring, while the seven rows of marks occupied the wall to her left. Nothing blocked the exit except distance, the line, and what neither of them understood.

The sixth mark appeared. Orem flinched so hard his shoulder struck the passage wall.

Nari had never seen him do that. She started along the wall toward the end of the pale line, carrying the lamp in one hand and the slate under her other arm.

“Slowly,” Orem said.

“I am.”

“No, you're thinking. Move slowly.”

That made her angry enough to steady her. She took another step, watching the marks as much as the floor. They remained lit. When she reached the end of the pale line, she lowered the lamp to the floor and set the slate beside it, chalk secured in its groove.

For the first time since Orem entered, both her hands were empty.

“The seventh point,” she said.

“Leave it.”

“You've seen it before.”

It was not a question. She knew it from the way he would not look at her.

“Nari.”

“You knew something was wrong on the terrace.”

“I knew enough to tell you to stop.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No.” His voice broke on the word, and he looked furious about it. “No, it isn't.”

A pressure passed through the floor. Nari felt it through both sandals, a slow upward push too broad to feel like vibration, and the lamp flame trembled at her feet. Orem bent his knees as though the floor had shifted.

“You felt that.”

“Yes.”

“Before?”

“Not like this.”

The seventh mark appeared, and Orem went white.

All seven now burned beneath the seven rows. For one suspended moment Nari's mind tried to make that completion satisfying: a set finished, a sequence closed. Then the pressure came again, deeper this time, and her stomach tightened as though she had missed a stair in the dark.

“Orem.”

“Come here.”

Nari remained beside the lamp and slate at the end of the pale line. Before she could move, the first mark dimmed and then the second. They did not go dark; their brightness changed one after another, traveling toward the seventh.

At the center of the chamber, the amber reflection of her lamp shifted across the God-Stone. A second line did not.

Nari bent toward the slate without touching it. Her terrace drawing showed the displaced star. The wall showed the displaced point. The line in the black surface was not the same shape, but the wrongness was there again.

Her first reaction was relief. There. A relation. Something she could test.

The relief lasted only long enough for her to recognize it. Then fear came in behind it.

“Orem, you saw this.”

“I saw enough.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means I was stupid.”

“That doesn't answer me.”

“I know.”

He came farther into the chamber, ignoring his own warning until his toes were almost at the pale floor line. “I came down here when I was younger. Not alone. Not the first time, either. We found things we couldn't explain, so we came back. Of course we came back. We measured. We argued. We told ourselves that if we were careful, careful meant safe.”

Nari stared at him. “Who was with you?”

“Not now.”

“Who?”

“Dead, gone, old enough to have forgotten on purpose—I don't know, Nari.” His hands were shaking. He closed them, opened them, then gave up trying to hide it. “That is the point. I don't know anymore.”

The marks changed, all seven this time, and the pale line inside the God-Stone changed with them. Nari's attention snapped back to it.

Orem swore again.

“What happened when you came down?”

“Nothing I could prove.”

“What happened?”

“We disagreed about that too.” He looked at the marks with naked hatred. “One man said the chamber had answered him. Another said it only repeated what we brought into it. I thought they were both making stories out of frightened eyes. Then I saw something I couldn't fit to either account.”

“What?”

“I don't know.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because it is true!”

His shout struck the chamber and died without an echo. Orem stood breathing hard for a moment, then rubbed both hands over his face.

“I stopped coming down,” he said. “Then I stopped reading the old accounts. Then I stopped asking the keepers what they had seen. Each time it felt sensible. Each time I could give a reason.” He lowered his hands and looked at her. “Twenty years later I have reasons and almost no knowledge.”

The seventh mark narrowed until every other mark had gone dark. Only the displaced point above the final row remained visible, ringed in pale light.

“Come here,” Orem said. “Now.”

She looked at the open passage behind him, then at the pale line between them. “Do you know what happens if I cross?”

“No.”

“If I stay?”

“No.”

The answers came so quickly that they frightened her more than hesitation would have.

“Then which is safer?”

“I don't know!” His face twisted. “I am not hiding an answer from you. I don't have one. I spent years treating not knowing as if it meant nothing could happen. I was wrong. That doesn't make this safe.”

The floor pressed upward again. Nari's calves tightened until they hurt, and she had to force her knees loose. The pale line between them brightened, not enough to illuminate the chamber but enough to erase any doubt about where it lay.

Orem backed one step away from it. Nari stayed beside the lamp and slate, hands still empty.

“Come around the end. We leave.”

“And then?”

“We get out of this room.”

“Does leaving stop this?”

Orem opened his mouth, then closed it. “I don't know.”

Nari looked at the seven rows.

“Nari, please.”

She almost answered him with another question. She could feel it forming, neat and sharp, something she could use to force one more distinction out of him. Her teeth hurt from clenching them.

She let the question go.

“I don't know what to do,” she said.

Orem's anger went out of him. “Neither do I.”

For the first time since he entered, they sounded like two people in the same room.

The pale floor line dimmed. Nari's stomach dropped with it. Nothing replaced the light for one heartbeat, then two.

The God-Stone pulsed.

Colorless light passed through its lower surface, and Nari flinched backward before she could stop herself. Her heel struck the lamp base. It rocked once but did not fall. The displaced point vanished, but the pale line within the God-Stone remained.

“Come away,” Orem whispered.

Nari took one step around the end of the line toward him, leaving the lamp and slate just behind her.

Then the answering light came.

It began along the lower surface of the God-Stone, thin and colorless, no brighter than moonlight through cloud, and spread upward and outward without throwing the shadows a lamp should have made. The chamber did not become brighter. It became harder to understand.

The far wall snapped into such impossible clarity that Nari could see a grain of pale dust beneath the seventh row as sharply as the blue stitching at Orem's cuff. She looked from one to the other without the small change her eyes always made between near work and distant sighting. The passage behind him remained where it had been, but she could see the first stair and the turn above it together, one beyond the other without the expected loss of detail.

Nari shifted her head a finger's width. Orem's shoulder should have moved against the wall behind him. The angle barely changed. Her eyes watered. She blinked hard, then again, but nothing corrected itself.

Seven marks returned at once. Pressure rose through the floor. The line in the God-Stone moved.

Nari reached back toward the slate without looking and struck the lamp instead.

“Orem—the line moved first.”

“What?”

“No. The marks did.”

“Nari.”

She looked again. The marks changed together and the line followed them, unless it anticipated them. She could not tell. Her heart hammered so hard it broke the count she had started in her head.

One, two. The floor pressed. Three—

The seventh point flared.

No. That had happened before three.

She started again and lost it sooner. Too much was happening at once. Her mind kept choosing an order and the next instant refused it.

Nari dropped into a crouch beside the lamp and caught the slate with both hands. The chalked star shook beneath her fingers. Wrong-moving star. Displaced point. Moving line. There was a relation between them. She knew there was.

She could not tell what it was.

That frightened her more than the light.

“Orem, I can't—”

She stopped because she had been about to say she could not measure it.

As if measurement were the emergency.

Orem stood near the passage with one hand over his mouth. His face was wet. He was crying and seemed not to know it, and the sight of that frightened her in a different way than anything the chamber had done.

Then the room broke another rule.

Nari looked toward the left corner, where the marked wall should have met the side wall in a hard vertical seam. The seam was still there. So was the lamp's weak oval on the floor below it. But when she shifted sideways, the near edge of the God-Stone should have slid across that seam. It did not. Stone, seam, and black edge held their places against one another as though they lay on the same flat surface.

She moved her head again. Nothing separated. The corner still looked like a corner, yet the usual change in angle never came.

There was no voice in the light, no face, no god-shape, nothing she could point to and name. There was only correspondence without explanation: wall, floor, black surface, and the star she had seen above the city. For one breath they seemed parts of the same statement, though Nari had no idea what the statement said.

Then the light narrowed.

Far within the God-Stone—or somewhere her eyes could not place—a point brightened.

Nari looked down at the slate. She wanted to write, to trap the moment in chalk before it changed, to mark one sequence and then another until terror became procedure and procedure became something she could defend.

The first rule she had written downstairs had survived less than a minute.

She still wanted another.

That frightened her.

Nari lowered the chalk and waited for a statement she could defend.

None came.

The answering light remained.
