# The Answering Light

Another sandal fell on stone, and the fourth white mark appeared.

Nari kept her eyes on the wall as Orem came down the last steps. He stopped where the passage opened into the chamber, one hand against the stone. The fifth mark appeared before either of them spoke.

“Come away from there,” he said.

“I’m not across the line.”

“Come away anyway.”

Nari looked down. The pale strip crossed the floor several paces behind her. Her heel had touched it once after the rim appeared, but she had moved away, and the first mark had gone dark. Then the marks had begun returning as Orem descended.

“The rule changed,” she said.

His gaze moved from her feet to the wall. “What rule?”

Nari lifted the slate. “When I crossed the line, the first mark appeared. If I stayed across it, more followed. If I stepped back, they went dark. I repeated it.” When his mouth tightened, she added, “I wrote down only what happened. I didn’t name it.”

The sixth mark appeared.

Orem went still.

Nari heard her thumb scrape the slate frame. The chalked lines seemed suddenly small beside the wall, but they were still what she had seen. “I thought I had found a pattern.”

“You found enough.”

“Enough for what?”

“To leave.”

The passage behind him was open. Nothing blocked them. That should have made the answer simple, but Nari looked back at the wall instead. The seventh point above the final row remained displaced, its pale rim brighter than before. Six marks glowed below. The black surface of the God-Stone hung through the ceiling at the center of the chamber, catching the lamp as a narrow amber streak.

“Orem. Look at the seventh point.”

“I have.”

“Before?”

He did not answer.

“You knew something was wrong on the terrace.”

“I knew you should stop.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No.”

A faint pressure passed through the floor. Nari felt it through her sandals, and the lamp flame trembled though no draft touched her face. Orem looked down.

“You felt that.”

“Yes.”

“Have you felt it before?”

He drew a breath. “Not here.”

Every apprentice knew the old warnings: lower ways were closed, certain chambers were not entered, certain questions were better left with older men.

“Do you know why the warnings exist?” Nari asked.

“Some of them.”

“Did you see something?”

“Once, I thought every unanswered question was an invitation.”

Nari almost smiled. “You?”

His eyes flicked toward her. “Yes. Me.”

The seventh mark appeared.

They both turned.

Seven.

The pressure in the floor deepened and then eased. Orem stepped into the chamber. “Come here.”

This time Nari moved toward him, but only two paces. The marks stayed lit.

“What happened?” she asked.

He rubbed one hand over the other. “Nothing I can give you as a rule.”

“I didn’t ask for one.”

“No.” His gaze dropped to the slate. “I saw things I could not make fit. I heard accounts from people I trusted that did not agree with each other.”

He stopped.

“And then?”

Orem looked back at the wall. “I stopped asking before I knew.”

The first mark dimmed, then the second. Nari turned sharply. They did not go out; their brightness changed one after another, moving from the first toward the seventh.

At the center of the chamber, the amber streak on the God-Stone shifted.

Nari picked up the lamp and moved it sideways. The reflection followed, but a second, much paler line did not.

“Orem.”

“I see it.”

She set the lamp down and brought up the slate. Her drawing from the terrace was crude, but the displacement of the seventh star was clear. She looked from the chalk to the pale line on the God-Stone and back again.

Not a copy. Not a picture. But the wrongness repeated.

For a moment the recognition pleased her.

Then it did not.

“Nari,” Orem said, “leave it.”

“I’m not touching anything.”

“You are still here.”

“So are you.”

The marks continued their slow change. The pale line on the God-Stone shifted again.

Nari looked toward the passage. “Do you know what happens if we leave?”

Orem said nothing.

That was answer enough.

The seventh mark narrowed, and every other mark went dark. Only the displaced point remained visible on the wall, circled by its pale rim, while the thin line on the God-Stone held steady.

Orem stepped toward the passage. “Now.”

Nari almost followed.

Then the pale floor line behind her brightened.

She turned. It was the same line she had crossed before, now visible without the lamp.

Orem saw it. “Do not cross it.”

“I’m already on this side.”

“Then stay there.”

Earlier, crossing the line had begun the sequence and stepping back had ended it. Now the sequence had reached seven without her.

Nari looked at Orem.

“Were you safer?” she asked.

His eyes lifted.

“For stopping?”

He looked toward the God-Stone. “For a time.”

“And now?”

A long silence passed before he answered.

“I have spent years telling myself that what I did not know could not matter.”

The chamber pressed faintly against the soles of her feet again.

“That does not mean you should make the opposite mistake,” Orem said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Nari looked at the pale line. “No.”

A sound escaped him, too short to be laughter.

The seventh mark contracted to a point, and the pale rim around the displaced point brightened. Nari set the slate beside the lamp.

“I’m not saying this is right.”

“Nari.”

“I don’t know what right is here.”

“Then stop.”

She looked back at him. “Does stopping leave it alone?”

Orem did not answer.

The pale line dimmed beneath her feet. For a moment nothing replaced it.

Then the God-Stone gave a faint pulse of colorless light.

Nari flinched. The displaced point on the wall went dark, but the pale line on the God-Stone remained.

“Come away,” Orem whispered.

Nari stayed where she was.

Then the answering light came.

It appeared along the lower surface of the God-Stone, thin at first, no brighter than moonlight through cloud. It spread without casting a clear edge, and the wall marks returned all at once: seven pale points beneath seven rows.

Nari stopped breathing until her chest hurt.

The light strengthened. The chamber did not brighten like a room with another lamp. Instead the far wall, the floor lines, and the opening behind Orem sharpened until distance itself became difficult to judge.

The line on the God-Stone shifted.

Nari looked down at her slate. The wrong-moving star was still there in chalk.

The relation held.

She knew that much.

The marks on the wall changed together. The line on the God-Stone changed with them. Something in the floor pressed upward through her sandals in a slow measure. Nari tried to follow it, and for several heartbeats she thought she could.

Then one change arrived before the one she expected, and the pattern in her mind fell apart.

She began again. Another correspondence held, then failed.

Or changed.

She could not tell which.

Orem stood beside the passage with one hand over his mouth. Nari looked at him, then back to the light gathering along the black surface. When she looked again, his hand had fallen.

His face was wet.

He was not looking at her. He was staring at the changing marks with an expression she could not name, but she remembered his words: I stopped asking before I knew.

For an instant Nari had the sensation that the chamber was larger than its walls.

She checked the passage, then the lamp, then Orem. Nothing had moved.

The sensation remained.

There was no voice in the light, no image she could name, no shape that resolved into anything she understood.

The wall, the floor, and the black surface above them held their impossible correspondence for another breath.

Then the light narrowed.

Far within the God-Stone—or somewhere her eyes could not place—a point brightened.

Nari reached for the slate.

Her fingers stopped above it.

She wanted to write that the pattern had answered. She wanted to mark what had changed and try again until the change yielded a rule.

Instead she remembered how easily the first rule had failed.

A colder fear moved through her then, not of being wrong, but of being right often enough to keep going.

She waited for a statement she could defend.

None came.

The answering light remained.
