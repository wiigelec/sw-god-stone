# The Answering Light

Orem appeared in the passage with one hand braced against the wall. The fourth white mark still shone. Nari stood beyond the pale floor line, closer to the marked wall and the God-Stone than to the exit. The shortest path to Orem crossed the line she had tested, and going around its end meant staying in the chamber longer.

Orem took in the distance between them, the line, and the marks on the wall, and swore. Not loudly. Not like a priest. Like a man who had dropped something heavy on his own foot.

“Don't cross that.”

Nari looked at the marks. “It didn't wait for you to cross.”

“I know.”

“Then the line isn't what starts it.”

“No.” His eyes stayed on the wall. “But I've seen it matter. Come around the end.”

“I already crossed it. Once. Then back.”

His face changed. “And it answered?”

“The marks started. They stopped when I crossed back.”

“Gods.”

The fifth mark appeared while neither of them was moving. Nari glanced at it, then back at Orem. “That isn't what it did before.”

“I can see that. Move.”

Orem stepped into the chamber, then stopped before the pale strip. The passage remained open behind him. Nothing blocked the exit except distance, the line, and whatever neither of them understood. The sixth mark appeared, and Orem flinched hard enough that his shoulder struck the wall. Nari had never seen him do that, and the sight sent her toward the end of the pale line before she had quite decided to move.

“Slowly,” Orem said.

“I am.”

“No, you're thinking. Move slowly.”

That made her angry enough to steady her. She took another step, watching the marks as much as the floor. They remained lit. When she reached the end of the line, she lowered the lamp to the floor and set the slate beside it, chalk secured in its groove. For the first time since Orem entered, both her hands were empty.

“The seventh point,” she said.

“Leave it.”

“You've seen it before.”

It was not a question. She knew it from the way he would not look at her, and when he finally said her name it sounded more like a warning than an answer. The refusal itself told her enough to keep pressing.

“Nari.”

“You knew something was wrong on the terrace.”

“I knew enough to tell you to stop.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No.” His voice broke on the word. “No, it isn't.”

A pressure passed through the floor. Nari felt it through both sandals, broad and slow, as if the stone beneath her had drawn breath. The lamp flame trembled, and Orem bent his knees. For a moment neither of them moved.

“You felt that.”

“Yes.”

“Before?”

“Not like this.”

The seventh mark appeared, and Orem went white. All seven now burned beneath the seven rows. For one suspended moment Nari's mind tried to make the completion satisfying: a set finished, a sequence closed. Then the pressure came again, deeper this time, and the satisfaction vanished with it.

“Orem.”

“Come here.”

The first mark dimmed, then the second. They did not go dark; their brightness changed one after another, traveling toward the seventh. At the center of the chamber, the amber reflection of her lamp shifted across the God-Stone while a second line remained fixed. That difference caught her attention harder than the light itself.

Nari bent toward the slate without touching it. Her terrace drawing showed the displaced star. The wall showed the displaced point. The line in the black surface was not the same shape, but the wrongness was there again. There was a relation, and for an instant that discovery brought relief before fear followed close behind it.

“Orem, you saw this.”

“I saw enough.”

“What does that mean?”

He came farther into the chamber, ignoring his own warning until his toes were almost at the pale line. “I came down here when I was younger.”

Nari watched him without answering. His eyes remained fixed on the wall, as though speaking to her directly would make the memory more difficult to hold. She had never seen him look uncertain in quite this way.

“Not alone.”

“With who?”

“Three others. At first.”

“At first?”

Orem shut his eyes. “We found things. We came back. We measured them.”

“I don't remember what we found.”

Nari stared at him.

“I remember arguing. I remember thinking everyone else was frightened and careless. I remember being certain I wasn't.”

The marks changed again, and Orem stared at them long enough that Nari wondered whether he had forgotten she was there. Something in his face tightened, then loosened. Whatever he was seeing now had found an old place in him.

“One man said the chamber answered him. Another said it only repeated what we brought into it.”

“And you?”

“I said they were making stories out of frightened eyes.”

“What did you see?”

His mouth opened, but nothing came out. When he finally looked at her, the silence had already become an answer.

“I don't know what happened. I don't know what I thought happened. I stopped coming down here.” He rubbed both hands over his face. “Then I stopped reading the old accounts. Stopped asking the keepers. Every time I had a reason.”

His hands fell to his sides. “Twenty years later I have reasons and almost no knowledge.”

The seventh mark narrowed until every other mark had gone dark. Only the displaced point above the final row remained visible, ringed in pale light, and Orem's attention fixed on it as if he expected something worse to follow. Nari found herself watching his face almost as closely as the wall.

“Come here,” he said. “Now.”

Nari looked at the open passage behind him, then at the line between them. “Which is safer?”

Orem followed her gaze. “I don't know.” The admission seemed to hurt him. “I spent years treating not knowing as if it meant nothing could happen. I was wrong. That doesn't make this safe.”

The floor pressed upward again, and the pale line brightened. Orem backed one step away from it, putting a little more distance between himself and the thing he had warned her not to cross. Nari stayed where she was.

“Come around the end. We leave.”

“And then?”

“We get out of this room.”

“Does leaving stop this?”

His mouth tightened. “I don't know.”

Nari looked at the seven rows, trying to find something in their arrangement that had not already changed beneath her attention. The displaced point remained lit. Nothing about it offered an answer.

“Nari, please.”

She almost answered with another question. She could feel it forming, neat and sharp, something she could use to force one more distinction out of him, but this time she let it go. The question remained in her mind anyway, useless and persistent.

“I don't know what to do,” she said.

Orem's anger went out of him. “Neither do I.”

For the first time since he entered, they sounded like two people in the same room. The pale floor line dimmed while they faced one another. Nothing replaced it for one heartbeat, then two, and in the dark interval the God-Stone pulsed.

Colorless light passed through its lower surface. Nari flinched backward, her heel striking the lamp base hard enough to make it rock once before settling. The light did not flare or spread, but the surface of the stone seemed somehow less solid than it had been a moment before.

“Come away,” Orem whispered.

Nari took one step around the end of the line toward him. For an instant nothing changed, and she almost believed the chamber might let them leave as easily as they had entered. Then the answering light came.

It spread across the lower surface of the God-Stone, thin and colorless, no brighter than moonlight through cloud. It threw no new shadows. The chamber did not become brighter; it became harder to understand. Nari felt that difference before she could decide what had changed.

The far wall snapped into impossible clarity. Nari could see a grain of pale dust beneath the seventh row as sharply as the blue stitching at Orem's cuff. She looked from one to the other without the small change her eyes always made between near work and distant sighting. The passage behind him remained open, but she could see the first stair and the turn above it together, one beyond the other without any loss of detail. Her eyes watered. She blinked, but nothing corrected itself.

Seven marks returned at once. The line inside the God-Stone moved, or perhaps the marks moved first. Nari stared until the line shifted again, and then the certainty reversed itself: no, the seventh point had brightened before it. She tried to hold the order in memory and felt it slipping even as she formed it.

She began counting. One, two—the floor pressed upward. Three—the line moved. The sequence seemed clear for half a heartbeat before she realized the line had already moved. She started again, but the marks changed together and the line followed them, unless it had anticipated them.

“Nari.”

She lifted one hand toward him without looking away. “Don't.”

“Don't what?”

“I don't know.”

Her heart hammered hard enough to break the count. She tried once more and lost the sequence sooner. Too much was happening at once. Her mind kept choosing an order and the next instant refused it. Every attempt to fix the sequence seemed to create a different one.

Nari dropped into a crouch beside the lamp and caught the slate with both hands. The chalked star shook beneath her fingers. Wrong-moving star. Displaced point. Moving line. There was a relation between them. She knew there was, and she could not tell what it was. That frightened her more than the light.

“Orem, I can't—”

She stopped because she had been about to say she could not measure it, as if measurement were the emergency. When she looked up, Orem stood near the passage with one hand over his mouth. His face was wet. He was crying and seemed not to know it.

Then the room broke another rule. Nari looked toward the left corner, where the marked wall met the side wall in a hard vertical seam. She shifted sideways, expecting the near edge of the God-Stone to move across it.

It did not. She moved her head back. Stone, seam, and black edge held their places against one another as though they lay on the same flat surface. The corner still looked like a corner, but nothing changed angle.

There was no voice in the light, no face, no god-shape, nothing she could point to and name. There was only correspondence without explanation: wall, floor, black surface, and the star she had seen above the city. For one breath they seemed parts of the same statement, though Nari had no idea what the statement said. The sense of meaning was almost worse than meaning itself.

The light narrowed, and far within the God-Stone—or somewhere her eyes could not place—a point brightened. Nari looked down at the slate. She wanted to write, to trap the moment in chalk before it changed, to mark one sequence and then another until terror became procedure and procedure became something she could defend. Her fingers found the chalk before she decided to reach for it.

She held it above the slate. The terrace marks were still there, followed by the chamber marks and the first rule she had written below them. Every line on the slate recorded something she had actually observed. The mistake had not been writing them down. The mistake had been believing that enough correct observations must eventually force the world to provide an answer.

Nari returned the chalk to its groove.

“Orem.”

He lowered his hand from his mouth.

“We leave.”

He stared at her, then nodded.

Nari picked up the lamp and tucked the slate beneath her arm. The answering light still moved across the God-Stone, but she did not try to decide whether the wall answered it or anticipated it. She followed Orem around the end of the pale line and into the passage. At the first stair he stopped and looked back.

“What do we tell them?”

Nari thought of the crowd dispersing above, the priests, the counters, the seven marks she had made before Orem told her to stop.

“What happened,” she said.

“And what it means?”

She tightened her arm around the slate. “We don't know what it means.”

They climbed.

Behind them the chamber remained lit, unanswered and unrecorded beyond the point where Nari's evidence ended. She did not mistake that for nothing.
