# Beneath the God-Stone

The sounds of the High Count followed Nari down the first flight of stairs. Already they were changing. The last bells gave way to voices, sandals on stone, benches scraping back against the terrace wall. Someone laughed. Someone called for a lamp at the western stair. Above her, the city was settling after a ritual everyone believed had ended as it should.

Nari kept one hand against the wall and the other pressed over the slate beneath her tunic. She knew these first turns well enough to descend in the weak light that reached down from the terrace. Apprentices used the upper service passages when the public ways were crowded or when lamps needed trimming before a ceremony. She had carried oil through them, hauled old cloth out through them, and once spent half a morning crawling under a landing after Orem dropped the bronze pin from a sighting frame.

At the third landing, a painted blue line crossed the wall from floor to ceiling, and beyond it the stair continued downward into darkness. Every apprentice knew the rule. The lower ways belonged to the keepers of the foundation: old supports, old drains, sacred spaces that had no business with sky-readers. No one crossed the blue line without instruction, and no one had ever instructed Nari.

She listened for footsteps. The voices above were softer now. A shallow wall niche beside the landing held three tallow dips for the foundation keepers, and after a moment she took one. Stealing temple light somehow felt like a smaller offense than crossing the line, which was absurd enough that she nearly put it back. Instead she struck the little flint kept on its cord beneath the niche. Two sparks died against the wick. The third caught, giving her a mean yellow flame and enough light to see the next turn.

Nari took out the slate. The seventh column was crowded where she had recorded the star more than once: one position, then another, then another. If she had made a mistake, she had repeated it carefully. Orem had told her to stop recording. He had not told her what she had seen was false.

She tucked the slate under her arm and crossed the blue line.

The stair narrowed almost at once. She turned sideways to pass a stack of unused lamp stands, then ducked beneath a shelf crowded with cracked bowls. Dust lay thick enough that her sleeve cut a clean streak through it when she brushed the wall. This part belonged to the temple she knew: neglected, inconvenient, ordinary.

Another turn changed that.

The lamplight moved strangely across the wall ahead, and Nari slowed. Upstairs, even the best-cut stone broke the light at its joints. Fine shadows caught along seams. Chisel work made tiny ridges shine and disappear as the flame moved. Here the light slid across the surface without interruption.

She brought the lamp closer. The wall shifted between gray and brown beneath the flame. She ran her fingertips over it, then her palm. It was cold and faintly slick, more like river stone polished by years of water than any wall she had touched in the temple, except that the surface did not curve beneath her hand.

She balanced the slate against her forearm and wrote:

**Lower wall. Surface continuous as far as lamp shows. Cold. Smooth.**

After considering it, she added:

**May be stone.**

That was as far as she was willing to go.

The passage continued downward. Temple shapes remained around her, though the farther she went, the less comfortably their familiar names fitted. One opening was roughly doorway-sized but narrowed toward the floor. Another rose well above her head while barely admitting her shoulders. A shallow channel beside her feet ran straight for several paces, then turned upward and climbed the wall without widening or wavering.

Nari followed it with the lamp until it vanished into the ceiling. Her first thought was that no mason could have done it. She scratched that thought out before it reached the slate and wrote instead:

**Have never seen stone cut this straight.**

The air grew cooler. The smell of soot diminished with each turn. When she touched a ledge below one of the strange openings, her fingers came away almost clean despite the dust choking the passages above.

Through another narrow opening, the passage gave way to a chamber large enough that her lamp could not reach the far wall.

A black shape descended through the ceiling.

For an instant the sight refused to fit together. Then the sheen of the surface became familiar.

On the public terrace, the God-Stone rose higher than three men, black against lamps and moonlight, its base hidden beneath offerings and prayer cords. Here, a surface with that same black sheen emerged from the ceiling and continued down into a low pale ring set into the chamber floor.

Nari approached slowly. Her little flame reflected as a narrow amber streak, but the reflection remained unnaturally clean as she moved. On ordinary stone it would have broken across pits and grain. Here it seemed to ride the surface.

On the slate she wrote:

**God-Stone continues below temple.**

She frowned, scraped out **God-Stone**, then wrote it again. Whatever else the thing might be, she had no other name for it.

Pale lines crossed the chamber floor in straight runs and abrupt turns. One began near the low ring around the God-Stone and ran toward the passage before stopping short of the wall. Others climbed the chamber walls and widened into flattened shapes. Seven of those shapes stood along the far wall, each with a vertical row of pale points above it.

Nari counted carefully.

Seven rows. Seven points in each.

At the final row she stopped.

The seventh point sat out of place.

She set the lamp on the floor and drew out her slate. The marks she had made on the terrace showed the same wrongness. The arrangements were not identical, but the relation held: six positions kept their expected order and the seventh broke it.

Her mouth had gone dry.

She swallowed, annoyed by the distraction, and copied the arrangement beneath her sky marks. The chalk clicked once against the slate because her fingers were damp. She wiped them on her tunic and tried again.

The chalk squeaked across the stone.

When she looked up, a milk-white line glowed beneath the first row and faded almost immediately.

Nari stayed still.

Nothing happened.

She shifted her stance to get closer and felt the edge of the pale floor marking beneath her boot. The line beneath the wall returned.

Nari looked down and moved her foot back. The wall went dark.

She stared at the wall, then at the strip in the floor. Her pulse had begun beating hard enough that she could feel it in the heel of the hand holding the slate.

One observation was an accident. Two might still be coincidence.

She crouched beside the floor line instead of stepping back and forth across it. Up close, the pale strip did not appear to sit on the surface. Tilting the lamp produced no edge. Rubbing it with her thumb left neither dust nor pigment.

Then she stood with both feet clear of it, fixed the wall in her sight, and crossed once. The first white mark appeared, and she remained where she was.

After several heartbeats, a second brightened beneath the next row. Then a third. Then a fourth.

Nari counted the intervals under her breath. They were not equal. She started again, trying not to hear how fast she was speaking.

The fifth began to pale into view. She stepped back across the line, and every mark vanished at once.

The sudden dark struck harder than the light had. That was enough; she did not repeat the test.

She wrote only what she could defend:

**Cross floor line: first white mark appears.**

**Remain across: more appear in sequence.**

**Cross back: all disappear.**

Below that:

**Cause unknown.**

The words steadied her because words could be checked. The chamber could not.

For several breaths she stood with the lamp at her feet and made herself look at the room as a room: passage behind her, pale floor line a few paces inside it, black God-Stone at the center, marked wall beyond. Nothing had moved. No door had closed. No voice had spoken.

The seventh point remained displaced.

Nari tucked the chalk into the slate's groove, picked up the lamp, and moved along the wall rather than across the floor line, following its end where it stopped short of the stone. She kept the lamp low and held the slate flat against her side while she circled farther into the chamber to inspect the final row from another angle. The pale marks remained dark.

That mattered.

Whatever crossing had done, merely being on this side of the chamber did not seem to do it.

She set the lamp down again before bending over the slate to compare the two drawings. That was when she noticed a pale rim around the displaced seventh point.

Nari straightened too quickly. The chamber tipped for half a heartbeat and settled again.

The rim had not been there when she copied the arrangement.

Or she had not noticed it.

Those were not the same claim.

She shifted the lamp. The rim weakened from one angle and returned from another, but it did not disappear.

A door closed somewhere above.

Nari looked toward the passage.

She had been below too long.

She tucked the chalk back into its groove, picked up the lamp, and started toward the passage with the slate under her arm, keeping to the wall so she would not cross the pale floor line again. Halfway around its end, a sandal struck stone somewhere up the stair.

Nari stopped. Another footstep followed, and the first white mark appeared.

She looked down. Both feet were well clear of the line, yet the mark remained.

The line was not the switch she had thought it was. Crossing it had coincided with the response; that did not make the crossing its cause. Whatever the chamber noticed, it could notice Orem before he entered.

A second sandal struck stone above, and the second white mark appeared.

Nari took one step toward the passage, then stopped herself before crossing the pale strip on the direct path. The route around its end was only a few paces longer. She could still leave.

The footsteps stopped.

“Nari.”

Orem's voice carried down the passage.

The third white mark appeared. The rule on her slate had lasted less than a minute.

“Orem?”

He descended another step, and the fourth mark came on with his footfall. Not after an interval and not after she moved. With him.

Nari pressed the slate against her ribs.

“Nari,” Orem called again, sharper now.

She looked from the lit marks to the dark mouth of the passage.

Recognition came before she wanted it—not of the chamber, but of his voice. He knew enough to be afraid.

Nari did not write that down.
