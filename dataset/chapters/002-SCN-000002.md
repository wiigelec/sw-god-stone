# Beneath the God-Stone

The sounds of the High Count followed Nari down the first flight of stairs. Already they were changing. The last bells gave way to voices, sandals on stone, benches scraping back against the terrace wall. Someone laughed. Someone called for a lamp at the western stair. Above her, the city was settling after a ritual everyone believed had ended as it should.

Nari kept one hand against the wall and the other pressed over the slate beneath her tunic. She knew these first turns well enough to descend in the weak light that reached down from the terrace. Apprentices used the upper service passages when the public ways were crowded or when lamps needed trimming before a ceremony. She had carried oil through them, hauled old cloth out through them, and once spent half a morning crawling under a landing after Orem dropped the bronze pin from a sighting frame.

At the third landing, a painted blue line crossed the wall from floor to ceiling, and beyond it the stair continued downward into darkness. Every apprentice knew the rule. The lower ways belonged to the keepers of the foundation: old supports, old drains, sacred spaces that had no business with sky-readers. No one crossed the blue line without instruction, and no one had ever instructed Nari.

She listened for footsteps. The voices above were softer now. A shallow wall niche beside the landing held three tallow dips for the foundation keepers, and after a moment she took one. Stealing temple light somehow felt like a smaller offense than crossing the line, which was absurd enough that she nearly put it back. Instead she struck the little flint kept on its cord beneath the niche. Two sparks died against the wick. The third caught, giving her a mean yellow flame and enough light to see the next turn.

Nari took out the slate. The seventh column was crowded where she had recorded the star more than once: one position, then another, then another. If she had made a mistake, she had repeated it carefully. Orem had told her to stop recording. He had not told her what she had seen was false. She tucked the slate under her arm and crossed the blue line.

The stair narrowed almost at once. She turned sideways to pass a stack of unused lamp stands, then ducked beneath a shelf crowded with cracked bowls. Dust lay thick enough that her sleeve cut a clean streak through it when she brushed the wall. This part belonged to the temple she knew: neglected, inconvenient, ordinary.

Another turn changed that. The lamplight moved strangely across the wall ahead, and Nari slowed to look. Upstairs, even the best-cut stone broke the light at its joints. Fine shadows caught along seams. Chisel work made tiny ridges shine and disappear as the flame moved. Here the light slid across the surface without interruption. When she brought the lamp closer, the wall shifted between gray and brown beneath the flame. She ran her fingertips over it, then her palm. It was cold and faintly slick, more like river stone polished by years of water than any wall she had touched in the temple, except that the surface did not curve beneath her hand.

She balanced the slate against her forearm and wrote:

**Lower wall. Surface continuous as far as lamp shows. Cold. Smooth.**

After considering it, she added:

**May be stone.**

That was as far as she was willing to go.

The passage continued downward. Temple shapes remained around her, though the farther she went, the less comfortably their familiar names fitted. One opening was roughly doorway-sized but narrowed toward the floor. Another rose well above her head while barely admitting her shoulders. A shallow channel beside her feet ran straight for several paces, then turned upward and climbed the wall without widening or wavering. Nari followed it with the lamp until it vanished into the ceiling. Her first thought was that no mason could have done it, but she scratched that thought out before it reached the slate and wrote instead:

**Have never seen stone cut this straight.**

The air grew cooler. The smell of soot diminished with each turn. When she touched a ledge below one of the strange openings, her fingers came away almost clean despite the dust choking the passages above. She looked back toward the stair, unsettled enough by the difference to note that too.

Through another narrow opening, the passage gave way to a chamber large enough that her lamp could not reach the far wall. A black shape descended through the ceiling, and for an instant the sight refused to fit together. Then the sheen of the surface became familiar.

On the public terrace, the God-Stone rose higher than three men, black against lamps and moonlight, its base hidden beneath offerings and prayer cords. Here, a surface with that same black sheen emerged from the ceiling and continued down into a low pale ring set into the chamber floor.

Nari approached slowly. Her little flame reflected as a narrow amber streak, but the reflection remained unusually clean as she moved. On ordinary stone it would have broken across pits and grain. Here it seemed to ride the surface. She raised the lamp and tried to see where ceiling met blackness, but the join was too close to follow.

On the slate she wrote:

**God-Stone continues below temple.**

She frowned, scraped out **God-Stone**, then wrote it again. Whatever else the thing might be, she had no other name for it.

Pale lines crossed the chamber floor in straight runs and abrupt turns. Some passed beneath the black central presence. Others climbed the walls and widened into flattened shapes. Seven of those shapes stood along the wall opposite her, each with a vertical row of pale points above it.

Nari counted carefully.

Seven rows. Seven points in each.

At the final row she stopped. The seventh point sat out of place.

She set the lamp on the floor and drew out her slate. The marks she had made on the terrace showed the same wrongness. The arrangements were not identical, but the relation held: six positions kept their expected order and the seventh broke it. She counted the wall once more and changed the lamp angle to make certain the pale point was not an accident of shadow. It remained where it was, so she copied the arrangement beneath her sky marks.

The chalk squeaked across the slate. When she looked up, a milk-white line glowed beneath the first row and faded almost immediately.

Nari stayed still and watched the wall. Nothing happened. She shifted her stance to get closer, and the line returned. This time she felt the edge of one of the pale floor markings beneath her boot. She looked down, moved her foot back, and watched the wall go dark. When she stepped across the marking again, the light returned.

That was enough to change the question.

Instead of stepping back and forth several more times, she crouched beside the floor line. Up close, the pale strip did not appear to sit on the surface. Tilting the lamp produced no obvious edge, and rubbing it with her thumb left neither dust nor pigment behind. She stood with one foot behind the line and studied the wall, then stepped across and remained there.

The first white mark appeared. Several heartbeats later, a second brightened beneath the next row, followed by a third and then a fourth. Nari watched the sequence long enough to know it was continuing, then stepped back. All the marks went dark together.

For several breaths she watched the empty wall. She had expected repetition to make the thing smaller, the way a wobbling sighting could become a mounting problem or a strange shadow could turn out to be smoke. Instead the repeated observation had exposed another behavior. She checked the slate and reduced what had happened to statements she could defend: crossing the pale line was followed by the first light; remaining there was followed by others; leaving made them disappear. Why any of that happened was still beyond her.

She wrote:

**Cross floor line: first white mark appears.**

**Remain across: more appear in sequence.**

**Step back: all disappear.**

Below that she added:

**Cause unknown.**

The phrasing sounded like something Orem might have written, and for a moment she could almost hear him telling her to sharpen the chalk before using it again. The thought steadied her.

She looked around the chamber. Her training had carried her this far without giving her a useful name for what she had found. Careful observation was supposed to clear confusion away, or at least that was how it had always seemed to her. Here each reliable result seemed to leave more of the room unexplained.

Nari returned to the wall. The seventh point was still displaced. The pale line on the floor still lay beneath her feet. The black surface of the God-Stone still caught the lamp in its thin amber streak. Those were things she could record. The other words arriving in her mind—warning, prayer, door, signal—brought assumptions with them, and she left them off the slate.

A door closed somewhere above, the sound faint after its long passage through stone. Nari looked toward the opening and realized how long she had been below. She picked up the lamp and started toward the passage, but after two steps a faint change on the far wall drew her back.

A pale rim now surrounded the displaced seventh point.

She checked her drawing. She had marked the point plainly, with nothing around it. She could not remember seeing the rim before, but that was not the same as knowing it had just appeared. Nari shifted the lamp and moved her head from side to side. The rim weakened from one angle and returned from another, but it did not disappear.

Backing toward the passage, she let her heel touch the pale floor line. The first white mark appeared beneath the first row. She moved forward again, and it vanished, while the rim around the seventh point remained.

A sandal struck stone somewhere up the stair.

Nari turned toward the passage. Another footstep followed, slow and deliberate. Someone was coming down. She thought first of the foundation keepers, then of Orem, and found little comfort in either possibility.

The footsteps stopped.

“Nari.”

Orem's voice carried down the passage.

She closed her eyes briefly. He did not sound angry. Anger would at least have told her what he thought she had done.

The pale rim around the seventh point brightened. Nari turned back toward the wall, and a second white mark appeared beneath the first row. She looked at her feet. She had not crossed the line.

Behind her, Orem descended another step. A third mark appeared.

“Nari,” he called again, sharper now.

She heard in his voice something she remembered from the terrace, when he had looked at her slate. It was not the irritation he used for careless work, and it did not sound like surprise. Recognition was the word that came to her.

Nari did not write it down.

Another sandal fell on stone, and the fourth white mark appeared.
